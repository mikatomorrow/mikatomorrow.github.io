Survey of Film Bulk Acoustic Resonators

Georgia Tech ECE — Interface IC Design  |  2025

⇩ open PDF in new tab

Abstract

As wireless communication systems become more prevalent, the need for high-performance MEMS resonator devices has greatly increased. FBARs offer a high quality factor (Q), small form factor, and compatibility with CMOS interface circuits, making them a popular choice for front-end applications in high-frequency filtering. This survey reviews the operating principles of FBARs, piezoelectric thin-film fabrication techniques, approaches for CMOS integration, and circuit-level interface architectures including ladder and lattice filter topologies.

I. Principles of Operation

FBARs are electromechanical transducers that convert electrical signals into bulk acoustic waves within a thin piezoelectric film via the inverse piezoelectric effect. The piezoelectric layer expands and contracts in response to an applied electric field, launching a longitudinal acoustic wave normal to the film plane. The resonant frequency is set by film thickness:

fˇ  ≈  v / (2tₙ)

where v = acoustic velocity in the piezoelectric material
      tₙ = piezoelectric film thickness

Material Selection

Common piezoelectric materials include AlN, ZnO, LiNbO₃, and PZT. Although ZnO has a larger longitudinal piezoelectric coefficient (d₃₃ = 11.67 pC/N vs. AlN’s 5.49 pC/N), AlN is the dominant material in FBARs due to its wider bandgap, superior CMOS compatibility, lower dielectric constant (reducing parasitic capacitance), and suitability for reactive sputtering deposition.

Equivalent Circuit — Butterworth-Van Dyke Model

A piezoelectric resonator is modeled by the BVD circuit: a motional branch (L, C, R in series) representing mechanical resonance, in parallel with C₀ representing static electrode capacitance. This model captures both the series resonance fˇ and the parallel anti-resonance fₐ, which together define the filter passband and stopband. The separation between fˇ and fₐ is characterized by the effective coupling factor kₜ².

II. Fabrication & CMOS Integration

FBARs are fabricated in two primary configurations:

Free-Standing FBAR — bulk micromachining etches an air cavity in the Si/SiO₂ substrate beneath the piezoelectric stack. Achieves higher Q and better acoustic isolation, but is more complex to fabricate. A notable process flow uses AlN deposition, ICP etching of Mo electrodes, sacrificial Si cavity, Au–Au wafer bonding, and substrate removal.
Solidly Mounted Resonator (SMR) — replaces the air cavity with a Bragg acoustic mirror: alternating layers of low- and high-acoustic-impedance materials (e.g., SiO₂/Mo–W) deposited on a Si substrate. Easier CMOS integration; Q is slightly lower due to imperfect acoustic confinement.

The dominant deposition technique for AlN films is reactive sputtering in nitrogen plasma.

III. Circuits & System Architecture

Ladder Filter

The most common FBAR filter topology. Series and parallel FBARs are arranged in a ladder network, with their anti-resonant and resonant frequencies precisely offset. The parallel FBAR anti-resonance (fₐᴙ) marks the passband onset; the series FBAR resonance (fˇˢ) marks its end. This creates a narrow, high-Q passband with deep notches on either side. Typical implementations use 4–8 resonator pairs.

Lattice Filter

An alternative topology using fewer resonators. The series resonant frequency of one FBAR branch is set equal to the parallel resonant frequency of the other, creating passband cancellation with notches at the band edges. The lattice filter produces a wider passband with less steep roll-off compared to the ladder filter, but with reduced component count.

Oscillator Integration

FBARs can replace the LC tank in a CMOS oscillator. Because FBARs have a much higher Q than on-chip inductors, FBAR-based oscillators achieve lower phase noise and lower power consumption without the negative conductance circuits required to sustain LC oscillation. FBARs are also used as mass, temperature, and pressure sensors via their resonant frequency sensitivity.

IV. Conclusion

FBARs provide compact, high-Q GHz filtering that is essential for modern wireless front-ends. AlN sputtering and acoustic mirror techniques enable reliable CMOS-compatible fabrication at scale. As the electromagnetic spectrum becomes more crowded and communication standards push to higher frequencies, concerns around linearity, parasitic capacitance, and MEMS–CMOS co-integration will become increasingly critical areas of development.
